§1The one‑slide version

Llama‑3.1‑8B represents cyclic concepts (months, days, hours) as circles in activation space. You might guess it does modular addition over those circles. It doesn't. It does ordinary base‑10 addition first, then re‑projects onto the appropriate circle in late layers.

"What month is six months after August?"
Input
circular geometry
six, August
→
Layer 18 MLP
6 + 8 = 14
base‑10, Fourier features
→
Output
14 → February
re‑project to circle

Three big claims, each backed by a different intervention method:

  1. The computation lives at the last‑token MLP of layer 18 (causal abstraction / DAS, §3).
  2. The intermediate is a number, not a month (cross‑task activation patching, §4).
  3. It is implemented by 28 MLP neurons (0.2%) writing to Fourier directions of periods T∈{2,5,10,20,50,100} (probes + ablations, §5–6).

§2Why this question is interesting

Prior work showed two pieces independently:

  • Small transformers trained on modular arithmetic learn Fourier features sitting on circles (Nanda 2023, Zhong 2023).
  • Large LMs represent real‑world cyclic concepts — months, weekdays, hours — on circles too (Engels 2025, Park 2025).

The seductive next sentence is: "...therefore the LM must be doing modular arithmetic on those circles." That would be a clean story where representation geometry dictates the algorithm. The paper's central finding is that this story is wrong. Geometric structure ≠ algorithmic structure.

Frame for your reading Every method in the paper exists to distinguish these two hypotheses:
H1 (rejected) The model rotates around the months‑circle by 6, lands on February.
H2 (supported) The model computes 6+8=14 in a numerical Fourier basis, then maps 14→February.

An odd corroborating fact

Llama is shockingly bad at explicit modular arithmetic ("what is 14 mod 12?"). It is competent at "six months after August." That mismatch is the first hint that the cyclic tasks are not being solved by a mod operator — they're being solved by something the model uses for many things at once: plain addition.

§4Distributed Alignment Search — where is the computation?

Before asking how the model adds, you have to find where. The paper uses Distributed Alignment Search (DAS, Geiger 2023) — an interpretability technique that searches for a low‑dimensional subspace of the residual stream that causally stores a particular variable.

Primer: what is a low‑rank matrix and why does it matter here?

The whole DAS technique hinges on one linear‑algebra object: a tall, skinny matrix $R \in \mathbb R^{d_{\text{model}} \times k}$ where $k \ll d_{\text{model}}$. Llama's residual stream is $d_{\text{model}} = 4096$ dimensions wide; DAS picks $k = 8$ or $k = 16$. That's the "low‑rank" part.

Think of it geometrically. The residual stream is a 4096‑dimensional vector space. A vector $h$ in it can be decomposed into "parts" along whatever basis you choose. $R$ is a recipe for picking out a $k$‑dimensional slice of that space — like picking a 2D plane in your 3D room. The columns of $R$ are an orthonormal basis for that slice, which means:

  • $R^\top h$ projects $h$ down to its $k$ coordinates inside the slice. (It strips out everything else.)
  • $R(R^\top h)$ takes those $k$ coordinates and re‑embeds them back into the full 4096‑dim space. The result is "$h$, but with every component outside the slice zeroed out."
  • $h - R(R^\top h)$ is the part of $h$ that lives orthogonal to the slice — the leftover.

The "orthonormal" qualifier ($R^\top R = I_k$) is what makes this clean: no stretching, no shearing. Projecting and re‑embedding is just "look at the slice."

Visual: the slice $R$ picks out

Left: a vector $h$ in the full residual stream (only 3 of 4096 axes shown). Middle: the $k$‑dim subspace spanned by $R$ — pictured as a 2D plane. Right: $R(R^\top h)$ is $h$'s shadow on that plane; the dashed leftover is what DAS leaves untouched.

Why "low rank" matters for the paper specifically

  1. It encodes a hypothesis. Saying "the input month lives in a $k=8$ subspace" is a strong, falsifiable claim: only 8 of 4096 directions matter for that variable. If DAS finds an $R$ with high IIA at low $k$, the variable really is concentrated; if it needs $k$ close to $d_{\text{model}}$, the "variable" was probably never a clean object.
  2. It makes the intervention surgical. The patch $h_o + R(R^\top h_c - R^\top h_o)$ only swaps the $k$ coordinates inside $R$. The other $4088$ dimensions stay exactly as they were in the original prompt. Without the low‑rank structure you'd be swapping the whole hidden state, which would tell you nothing about which variable you moved.
  3. It makes "shared mechanism" provable. The paper independently trains $R_{\text{months}}$, $R_{\text{weekdays}}$, $R_{\text{hours}}$, $R_{\text{add}}$, then measures how much their column spans overlap (App. D.4). High overlap between two low‑rank slices is meaningful; high overlap between two 4096‑dim "slices" would be vacuous.
  4. It connects to the neurons. The $\omega$ score in §8 — $\lVert R^\top d_i \rVert / \lVert d_i \rVert$ — is just asking "how much of neuron $i$'s output direction lives inside the subspace $R$?" That question only makes sense because $R$ is low‑dimensional; in a 4096‑dim "subspace" every neuron would score 1.
  5. It is what "distributed" means in Distributed Alignment Search. The variable is not localized to a single neuron or a single dimension — it's spread (distributed) across a small linear combination of dimensions. Low‑rank $R$ is exactly the mathematical object that captures "small linear combination."
Punchline Every causal claim in this paper — "input concept lives here", "the addition format is shared", "these 28 neurons write to the answer" — is mediated by a low‑rank $R$. The technique only buys you insight when $k$ is small enough that the slice is a meaningful part of the residual stream rather than "most of it."

The setup, plain English

You have a hypothetical causal model $\mathcal A$ that says: "There is a variable input_concept, a variable offset, and they combine into output_concept." You want to find, inside the 4096‑dimensional residual stream of Llama, a small subspace $R$ (with $k$ orthonormal columns) such that writing into $R$ reliably manipulates the variable you care about.

The intervention, in math

Given an original prompt $o$ and a counterfactual prompt $c$, replace the original hidden state $h_o$ with:

$$h = h_o + R\!\left(R^\top h_c - R^\top h_o\right)$$

This swaps only the part of $h$ that lives in $R$. Everything orthogonal to $R$ stays untouched. Then optimize $R$ end‑to‑end with the objective:

$$\mathcal L = \mathrm{CrossEntropy}\!\left(\;\mathcal N_{R\leftarrow \mathcal N(c)}(o),\; \mathcal A_{V\leftarrow \mathcal A(c)}(o)\;\right)$$

In words: "After I patch $R$ from $c$ into the forward pass on $o$, does the model behave as if its variable $V$ had been set to the value it has in $c$?" The metric is IIA — Interchange Intervention Accuracy.

Interactive: a DAS patch on the months task

If $R$ truly localizes input concept, patching just that subspace from "two months after April" into "six months after October" should make Llama predict two + October = December. The paper finds this works with >95% IIA when probing the residual stream right before the layer‑18 MLP, and drops sharply after it — meaning the model has "consumed" the input concept and produced the output by that point.

What DAS tells the paper

Input variables visible before L18 MLP

Input‑concept IIA reaches ~100% at the residual stream just before MLP 18. Offset is copied to the last token by layer 15.

Output appears after L18 MLP

Output‑concept IIA climbs to ~100% right after the same MLP. Input IIA drops by ~80 points across that sublayer.

The same pattern appears for all four tasks (months / weekdays / hours / pure addition) even though DAS was trained separately on each. Whatever circuit handles cyclic reasoning, it lives at L18 MLP, last token — and it's shared with plain addition.

Important non‑finding When the paper trains a circular probe (Engels 2025) on the residual stream at L18 / last token, no circle for the output is recoverable yet. Circles for the output concept only show up at layers 22–25. So the L18 MLP does its work in a space that is not the cyclic geometry. That's the first concrete fingerprint that "rotate around the circle" is the wrong story.

§5Cross‑task patching — the intermediate is a number

If the L18 representation is genuinely numerical, then it should be interchangeable between the addition task and the cyclic tasks. The paper does both directions.

Addition → Cyclic

Run 6+8=. Take the activation at L18 (within the shared DAS subspace) and splice it into "What month is X months after Y?". Prediction: the model outputs February (because 14 mod 12 = 2).

Result ≥40% accuracy on all three cyclic tasks; matches clean performance for months. Crucially, the model does not output the number "14" — the late layers convert it.

Cyclic → Addition

Run "six months after August". Splice the L18 activation into an arithmetic prompt a+b=. Prediction: the model outputs 14.

Result Clean diagonal: prompts with the same final month but different pre‑modulo sums (e.g. 3 and 15 → March) produce different patched outputs. The intermediate distinguishes 14 from 26, even though both map to February.

The "+100 echo" — a free piece of evidence

When patching cyclic→addition, the probability of 14 spikes, but so does the probability of 114. Sometimes the latter wins. Interpretation: the cyclic tasks never need a hundreds‑place representation (sums are always small), so the Fourier features with period 100 are causally inactive — leaving the hundreds‑digit ambiguous in vocabulary space. This is exactly what you'd predict if the intermediate is "a number written in base‑10 Fourier features with the high periods unused."

Why this is strong evidence Two completely different output distributions (months vs. integers) become interchangeable at L18 via a single shared subspace. That subspace cannot be encoding months (it would never produce "14") and cannot be encoding just integers (it produces "February"). It is encoding the pre‑modulo numerical sum, which both downstream pipelines can decode.

§6Fourier probes — the format of the intermediate

If the intermediate is a number, in what basis? Following Kantamneni & Tegmark and Zhou et al., the paper looks for Fourier features: linear directions in the residual stream that encode $\sin(2\pi n / T)$ and $\cos(2\pi n / T)$ for various periods $T$.

Primer: what is a Fourier feature?

Start with the classical idea. Joseph Fourier (1807) showed that any well‑behaved periodic function can be written as a sum of sines and cosines at different frequencies. The pair $(\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$ is the atomic unit: every other periodic shape is a weighted combination of those at various periods $T$.

The same pair $(\sin, \cos)$ is also the natural way to encode "where on a circle" you are. Given an integer $n$ and a period $T$, define the phase $\theta_T = 2\pi n / T$. Then the point $(\cos\theta_T,\,\sin\theta_T)$ sits on the unit circle, and it rotates by a constant angle whenever $n$ increases by 1. Two integers that differ by exactly $T$ land on the same point — i.e. this encoding is $n \bmod T$, but stored as 2D coordinates instead of a remainder.

In one sentence A Fourier feature for period $T$ is a pair of directions in activation space such that, when you project a hidden state onto them, you read off $\sin\theta_T$ and $\cos\theta_T$ for the number the model is "thinking about" — a 2D circular dial that tracks $n \bmod T$.

Why a neural network would invent this

Three reasons this is the right shape for arithmetic, all of which the paper invokes:

  1. Addition becomes rotation. If you store $n$ as $(\cos\theta_T,\sin\theta_T)$, then "add $b$ to $n$" is just "rotate by $b\cdot 2\pi/T$". The model only needs to learn one rotation operator per period, not a lookup table over every $(n,b)$ pair. This is the trick small transformers spontaneously learn on modular arithmetic (Nanda 2023, Zhong 2023).
  2. It's linear. Sine and cosine of $n$ are linear functions of $n$'s one‑hot — they can be written as a single weight vector dotted with $h$. Neural networks have many linear maps for free, so this representation is cheap.
  3. Multiple periods uniquely identify an integer. Knowing $n \bmod 2$, $n \bmod 5$, and $n \bmod 10$ doesn't quite pin $n$ down, but adding $T=20, 50, 100$ does — up to $\mathrm{lcm}$ of the periods. This is the Chinese remainder theorem, in geometric form: a handful of small circles together encode every integer in a long range.

Why base‑10 periods $\{2,5,10,20,50,100\}$?

Numbers in text are written in base‑10. A token like "47" is a single discrete piece, but the model sees adjacent tokens — "46", "48" — and has to relate them. The cheapest way to pick up "ones digit" and "tens digit" structure is to track residues at periods that divide 10 and 100. Period 2 catches parity. Period 5 catches "ends in 0 or 5." Period 10 catches the full ones digit. Periods 20, 50, 100 reach further. The model never invents period 7 or period 12 because no token co‑occurrence pressure pushes it to.

That is the central surprise of the paper: a months task seems like it should need period 12, but the cyclic representation Llama actually uses at L18 is the base‑10 set inherited from generic arithmetic — and the "→ February" step is a separate downstream lookup, not modular arithmetic on a months‑circle.

Concretely, inside Llama For each period $T$ and layer $\ell$, the Fourier feature is a pair of vectors $(w^{(\ell,T)}_{\sin}, w^{(\ell,T)}_{\cos}) \in \mathbb R^{4096}$. The probe asserts: "there exist these two directions in the residual stream such that $\langle w_{\sin}, h\rangle \approx \sin\theta_T$ and $\langle w_{\cos}, h\rangle \approx \cos\theta_T$ for whatever integer $h$ encodes." If the MSE fits, the feature exists. If steering along $w_{\sin}, w_{\cos}$ changes the output, the feature is causal.

Training a Fourier probe

For each period $T$, learn two affine probes (one sine, one cosine) from the layer‑$\ell$ last‑token activation $h^{(\ell)}_{a+b}$ of an addition prompt:

$$\min\;\mathrm{MSE}\!\left(\langle w^{(\ell,T)}_{\sin}, h^{(\ell)}_{a+b}\rangle + b^{(\ell,T)}_{\sin},\; \sin\!\tfrac{2\pi(a+b)}{T}\right)$$
$$\min\;\mathrm{MSE}\!\left(\langle w^{(\ell,T)}_{\cos}, h^{(\ell)}_{a+b}\rangle + b^{(\ell,T)}_{\cos},\; \cos\!\tfrac{2\pi(a+b)}{T}\right)$$

Sweep $1 \le T \le 150$. Only $T \in \{2,5,10,20,50,100\}$ — the base‑10 periods — fit well. These are the same periods Kantamneni & Tegmark found in standalone arithmetic, and they emerge in middle layers, consistent with DAS.

The kicker

The paper takes those probes — trained only on the addition task — and applies them to L18 activations from the cyclic tasks. The recovered sine‑cosine values trace clean circles with the same base‑10 periods. The months task does not get its own period‑12 representation. The weekdays task does not get a period‑7 one. They all inherit {2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100}.

§7Steering — turning probes into causal handles

Probes are correlational. To prove these Fourier features cause the cyclic outputs, the paper steers them: it writes a counterfactual value $n'$ into the residual stream by shifting along the sine‑cosine probe directions, while keeping the radius the model originally produced.

Given target $n'$, define the desired phase $\theta_T^{n'} = n'\cdot 2\pi/T$. Read off the original sine/cosine projections $\hat s, \hat c$ and the empirical radius $r_T = \sqrt{\hat s^2 + \hat c^2}$. Then patch:

$$\tilde h^{(\ell)} \leftarrow h^{(\ell)} + \big(\alpha r_T \sin\theta_T^{n'} - \hat s\big)\hat w_{\sin}^{(\ell,T)} + \big(\alpha r_T \cos\theta_T^{n'} - \hat c\big)\hat w_{\cos}^{(\ell,T)}$$

Stack this across all relevant $T$. Use $\alpha=10$ for strength. The paper finds that steering with probes trained on addition reliably changes the cyclic outputs to the predicted concept — a clean diagonal in the steered‑output heatmap.

Why $\alpha=10$ matters Needing a strong amplification suggests the Fourier features are part of the story but not the whole thing — there's likely additional redundancy / scaffolding. Worth flagging as a limitation if anyone asks.

Interactive: steering one period at a time

The arrow shows the steering delta in the (cos, sin) plane for period T. In Llama, this delta is rotated into the residual stream via $\hat w_{\sin}^{(T)}$ and $\hat w_{\cos}^{(T)}$. Slim shift in one period; combined across periods, this re‑addresses the entire integer.

§8The 28 neurons that do the addition

Now the deepest question: which parameters implement this Fourier‑basis addition? The paper finds a sparse group of 28 MLP neurons in layer 18 (0.2% of that layer's neurons) that do almost all the work, and partitions them into orthogonal clusters by period.

The MLP, in case it's been a while

Llama uses a gated MLP (Shazeer 2020). For neuron $i$ with gate, up, and down weights $g_i, u_i, d_i$:

$$\mathrm{MLP}(h) = \underbrace{(\mathrm{SiLU}(W_{\mathrm{gate}} h)\odot W_{\mathrm{up}} h)}_{\text{neuron activations}}\,W_{\mathrm{down}}$$

Each neuron has a single "down direction" $d_i$ — the row it writes into the residual stream when it fires.

Identifying the addition neurons

For each task, find neurons whose down‑direction lands inside the DAS output subspace $R_{\text{task}}$. Score:

$$\omega_{\text{task}}(d_i) = \frac{\lVert R_{\text{task}}^\top d_i \rVert}{\lVert d_i \rVert}$$

This is just "what fraction of $d_i$'s length sits inside the causally important subspace?" Threshold at $\omega > 0.4$. Only a handful of neurons clear the bar — and they are the same neurons across tasks (Pearson $r \ge 0.7$ between any pair of task scores).

Translation: those 28 neurons are almost the entire L18 contribution to this computation.

Why they cluster by period

Two parallel pieces of evidence partition the 28 neurons into disjoint clusters of periods T ∈ {2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100}:

  1. Activation periodicity. Average a neuron's activation over all addition prompts grouped by output sum. Neurons in the period‑T cluster fire with period $T$ in the sum (Fig 8a).
  2. Write direction. The down‑projection $d_i$ of each neuron has highest cosine with the Fourier probe pair $(w_{\sin}^{(T)}, w_{\cos}^{(T)})$ that matches its activation period (Fig 8b). Across clusters the $d_i$'s are essentially orthogonal.

Interactive: how a cluster computes its period

Each colored arrow is one neuron's down‑direction $d_i$ scaled by its activation on the prompt. The thick gray line is their sum. The star is the ground‑truth phase $\theta_T = (a+b)\cdot 2\pi/T$. A cluster of neurons in the same period builds the correct sum vector by combination — distributed but tidy.

Split vs. mixed activation patterns

For each neuron, you can ask: does the gate read one input and the up read the other (a "split" neuron), or do both vectors read both inputs (a "mixed" neuron)? Because the gate and up are element‑wise multiplied, split neurons are an elegant trick for combining two summands from different subspaces. 17/28 neurons split for cyclic tasks; for pure addition all 28 are mixed (because the input arguments are more entangled in arithmetic prompts).

§9Why use several periods? Parity neurons sharpen.

Why bother with $T=2$? Period 100 alone can already distinguish every integer in 0…99. The paper finds that smaller periods sharpen the prediction. Two neurons carry the parity signal:

  • n10297 — "odd neuron", fires for even+odd = odd.
  • n1712 — "even neuron", fires for even+even = even (and writes in the opposite direction in the output subspace).

Zero‑ablate just these two and the model's output distribution "blurs" — probability mass leaks to neighbors with the wrong parity. The cyclic decoder is fine; it just no longer knows whether to land on a +1 or -1 neighbor.

Mechanistic moral Coarse periods (2, 5) give cheap parity / residue checks that disambiguate fine periods (50, 100). It's the same logic as the Chinese remainder theorem, but distributed across orthogonal neuron clusters.

§10Limits and what's not done

  • One model. Everything is Llama‑3.1‑8B. The authors flag that Fourier features and circular concept representations appear in other models, but the specific 28‑neuron circuit is not promised to transfer.
  • One direction of cyclic task. Only forward offsets ("six months after"). No backward offsets, no distance queries.
  • The "→ February" step is unanalyzed. The paper isolates the addition step but does not open up the late‑layer decoder that maps 14 → February. That's explicitly future work.
  • Steering needs $\alpha=10$. Fourier features are necessary but possibly not sufficient — other downstream components reinforce the prediction.
  • Bigger‑period neurons may be missed. The 86% retention when only the 28 neurons are kept drops mostly on examples with large summands — suggesting the $\omega>0.4$ threshold filters out some genuine T=50/100 neurons.

§11Talking points for your reading

If you have to lead a discussion, here's the spine I'd hang it on:

  1. The hypothesis space. H1: rotate around the months circle. H2: integer add, then re‑project. Anyone could have predicted H1 from prior representation work; the paper's job is to rule it out.
  2. The four converging techniques. DAS localizes where (L18 last token). Cross‑task patching shows what format (integer, not month). Fourier probes show which basis (base‑10 periods). Neuron ablations + clustering show which parameters.
  3. The disqualifying observation. No circle is recoverable for the output at L18, even though the output has just been computed there. The cyclic decoder lives at L22+.
  4. The two‑step picture. Compute → re‑project. The paper handles compute; re‑projection is open.
  5. The broader claim. Representation geometry does not dictate algorithm. Models can reuse a generic mechanism across tasks even when the tasks "ought to" share geometry instead.
Things to push on
  • Is "the model represents this as an integer" a strong enough claim, or just "the residual stream subspace is interchangeable with the integer one at this layer"? (The intervention semantics are subtle.)
  • How robust is DAS to subspace dimension? The paper picks the best $k$ per task (8 or 16); the metric can be over‑fit. Appendix D matters.
  • Does the same picture hold for chain‑of‑thought / longer prompts, or only one‑shot cyclic Qs?
  • The +100 echo: what would fail if it weren't there? A useful tells‑me‑the‑story‑isn't‑a‑coincidence question.
